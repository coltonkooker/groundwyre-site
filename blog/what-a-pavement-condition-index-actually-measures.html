What a pavement condition index actually measures

PCI shows up in nearly every capital plan and council packet in the country, and outside of public works, almost nobody could tell you how the number is actually calculated. Here's the plain-language version.

If you've sat through a council meeting where a road project got approved or tabled, you've probably heard someone reference a "PCI score" without much explanation. Pavement Condition Index is a 0–100 rating that's become the closest thing the industry has to a common language for "how bad is this road, really" — but the way it's built is worth understanding, especially if you're the one explaining it to a resident.

It's not just about potholes

A visual inspection alone would just catch what's obvious at 20 miles an hour. A proper PCI assessment accounts for distress type (cracking, rutting, raveling, patching), severity (low, medium, high), and density — how much of the surface is actually affected, not just whether a problem exists somewhere on the segment.

Two roads can both have visible cracking and still land 30 points apart on the index, because one has isolated hairline cracks near the curb and the other has alligator cracking across the full travel lane.

Why the number decays the way it does

PCI doesn't fall in a straight line. A new road holds its score for years, then drops sharply once the surface layer fails and water starts reaching the base. That's the reason "we'll get to it next year" is such an expensive sentence in pavement management — a segment sitting at 45 today might be at 15 in eighteen months, and the repair cost at 15 is a full reconstruction instead of an overlay.

Where it fits into a prioritization decision

This is the part that's easy to get wrong: PCI tells you how bad a road is, not whether it should be fixed next. A cracked cul-de-sac with no traffic and a cracked arterial with two school bus routes can have similar scores and completely different urgency.

That's why in Groundwyre, condition is one factor feeding the Prioritization Engine alongside safety risk, cost efficiency, and equity impact — not the whole answer by itself. A council member asking "why this street" deserves an answer that includes the condition number, but doesn't stop there.

The plain-language version

If someone outside public works asks what PCI means, the honest short answer is: it's a standardized way of saying how much longer a road can go without failing, based on what inspectors can actually see and measure — not a guess, and not the whole story either.
