Why we made our AI show its work

Every priority score in Groundwyre comes with its reasoning attached. That decision made the model harder to build — and it's the reason planning teams trust it enough to actually use it in a council meeting.

When we started building the Prioritization Engine, the easy version was tempting: feed in condition data, traffic counts, and budget history, and output a ranked list. Fast to build, fast to demo, and — for a while — fast to sell.

It's also close to useless for the people we build for.

A public works director doesn't get to say "the algorithm said so" in a council meeting. They get asked, in front of residents, why Elm Street is getting repaved before Maple Avenue — and they have thirty seconds to answer in a way that holds up. If the honest answer is "I don't fully know, the model ranked it higher," that's not a tool. That's a liability with a nice interface.

What "explainable" means in practice

For every score the Prioritization Engine produces, we require four things to be true:

  • The score can be decomposed into its contributing factors — condition, risk, cost, equity — with a number attached to each.
  • A planner can see exactly how much each factor moved the final number.
  • A planner can adjust the weighting and watch the score recalculate live, without touching code or filing a support ticket.
  • Every override is logged, timestamped, and attributed — because six months later, someone in a public records request is going to ask why a score changed.

The performance we gave up

We won't pretend this was free. A more opaque ensemble model, tuned purely for predictive accuracy on historical repair data, outperforms our explainable scoring approach by a measurable margin in backtesting — somewhere in the range of 6–9%, depending on the asset class.

We made that trade deliberately. A model that's 9% more "accurate" against historical repair patterns but can't tell a city engineer why it ranked a bridge above a bus route isn't more useful — it's just more confident. And confidence without a reason attached is exactly the kind of AI language we try to avoid putting in front of the people using this software every day.

"If a city manager can't explain a decision to a city council, the tool has failed, no matter how accurate it was."

Where this leaves the model

In practice, explainability turned out to be less of a performance tax than we expected. Constraining the model to factor-decomposable scoring forced us to be more disciplined about what actually predicts infrastructure failure — and a few of the "black box" signals we dropped turned out to be proxies for things like ZIP code, which we didn't want influencing prioritization anyway.

The score you see in Groundwyre today isn't the most powerful model we could have shipped. It's the one we'd be comfortable defending in a room full of residents who pay the taxes that fund it.
